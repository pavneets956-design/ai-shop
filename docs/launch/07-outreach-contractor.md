## ⚖️ CASL — read this before sending a single message

Canada's Anti-Spam Legislation governs every commercial electronic message sent to or from Canada. Penalties run to $1M for an individual. Three things must all be true:

**1. You need consent — express, or implied.** You cannot email a business simply because you found their address.
- **Implied consent, conspicuous publication:** the address is published publicly (their website, a truck, a directory listing), it is **not** accompanied by a statement that they do not want unsolicited commercial email, and **your message is relevant to the business role that address represents.** A fence contractor's `info@` address, published on their own site, contacted about a business tool for contractors, is the case this template is written for. Keep a record — a screenshot or the URL and date — of where you found each address.
- **Implied consent, existing business relationship:** they bought from you within the last 2 years, or made an enquiry within the last 6 months. Record which one, and the date.
- **Express consent:** they said yes. Best, and rarest, in cold outreach.
- Implied consent from conspicuous publication does not expire on a fixed clock the way the 6/24-month relationship windows do, but it dies the moment they say stop.

**2. Every message must identify you.** Your name, the business name, a **mailing address**, and one more contact method (phone, email, or web) that stays valid for 60 days after sending.

**3. Every message must carry a working unsubscribe** that is easy to use, works for at least 60 days, and is actioned **within 10 business days** — that means without you doing anything, not "I will remember".

**Two more things specific to this business:**
- **Do not use an autodialler or an AI voice agent for cold outreach.** That is the CRTC's Unsolicited Telecommunications Rules and the National Do Not Call List, a separate regime with its own registration requirements and its own penalties. The outbound-calling code in this repository ships disabled for exactly this reason.
- ⟦**Owner action required before the first send:** CASL requires a real mailing address in the message. There is no address in any file in this project. Supply the business mailing address to use — a physical address or a valid PO box.⟧
- ⟦**Owner decision:** the domain `aibuiltbyhand.com` has no MX record, so a reply to any `@aibuiltbyhand.com` address bounces into nothing. Until that is fixed, send from and reply to the Gmail address. Sending business outreach from a personal Gmail costs some credibility — that is a real trade-off, and it is smaller than the cost of a reply that vanishes.⟧

## Sending rules

- **One at a time, by hand, from his own mailbox.** No mail-merge tool, no sequencer, no third-party sending platform. At this volume a personal send has a better reply rate and no deliverability risk.
- **Never send more than ~20 in a day** from a Gmail address, or the account gets throttled and the domain reputation suffers.
- **Keep a suppression list** and check it before every send. A second unsolicited email to someone who said no is the violation that gets reported.
- **One follow-up maximum**, 7–10 days later, three sentences, and only if the first got no reply at all. A reply of any kind — including "not interested" — ends the thread.
- **Log every send:** recipient, date, where the address was published (URL + screenshot), which consent basis applies, and the outcome. If a complaint is ever made, that log is the defence.
- **Personalise the first line.** Name the actual business and something specific from their site. A message that could have been sent to anyone reads as bulk, which is both worse-converting and worse-looking under CASL's "relevant to the recipient's role" test.
- **This is not legal advice.** If the volume grows past a handful a week, have a Canadian lawyer read the template once.
